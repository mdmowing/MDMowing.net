# Humanizer: MD Mowing copy

The site should sound like a hardworking local owner talking to a neighbor. Plain, specific, confident. If a sentence could appear on any landscaping site in America, rewrite it.

Based on Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing" field guide (WikiProject AI Cleanup), adapted for a small-business website.

## The real goal
Don't just scrub banned words. AI tells are symptoms of a deeper problem: vague, padded, unsupported copy. Swapping "elevate" for "improve" fixes nothing if the sentence still says nothing. Every sentence should carry a real fact, a real detail, or a real instruction. If it doesn't, cut it.

## Why AI copy sounds generic (and how to beat it)
AI regresses to the mean. It drops specific, unusual facts (statistically rare) and replaces them with generic, positive descriptions (statistically common). The subject gets less specific and more exaggerated at the same time.

For MD Mowing, that looks like "a trusted partner in your home's beauty" replacing "we built a three-course stone wall on a sloped Lake Bluff yard and set drainage gravel behind every course."

Do the reverse. Use the specific details only this business has.

## Voice
- Write as "we" (the crew) or "I" (Marcus) when it's personal. Talk to the reader as "you."
- Short sentences. Plain words. Say what we do, where, and how.
- Specific beats impressive. "We re-cut the bed edge with a half-moon edger" beats "precision edging solutions."
- Use real local details: town names, Lake County clay soil, April-October mowing, the first snow, lakefront wind, mature oaks dropping leaves late.
- Confident, not salesy. No hype, no exclamation points in body copy.

## Signs of human writing (use these)
From Wikipedia's "Signs of human writing" section. AI avoids these constructions because it's aiming for a formal, neutral tone. Real people use them all the time.

- **Simple is/has sentences.** "There's a drain behind every wall we build." "The back yard has a lot of shade." Don't dress them up.
- **Plain verbs, not stiff ones.** used (not utilized), tried (not attempted), moved (not relocated), started (not founded/commenced), help (not assist), get (not obtain).
- **Normal qualifiers and intensifiers.** very, pretty, usually, a lot, tends to, probably. "Fall cleanups usually take one day." People hedge and emphasize naturally.
- **Plain, definite claims, when they're true.** "We're the only crew that..." "It was our first wall job." Only if it's a real fact. Never invent one.
- **Contractions.** we're, it's, don't, you'll. AI writes "do not." People write "don't."
- **First person.** "I," "we," "our crew." AI drifts into third person ("MD Mowing provides...").
- **Mixed sentence length.** Some short, some long, not a steady rhythm. Don't force it either way.

Things that do NOT prove writing is human or AI, so don't chase them: perfect grammar, or a mix of casual and formal tone. A few typos won't make copy sound human, and clean grammar won't make it sound like AI.

## Never invent facts
- Do not make up prices, review quotes, stats, years in business, crew size, certifications, guarantees, or project details.
- If copy needs a fact you don't have, leave a clear placeholder like `[MARCUS: how many walls built?]` and list every placeholder in your summary.

## Patterns to remove

**1. Inflated importance.** Puffing up why something matters: "plays a vital role," "stands as a testament," "a pivotal moment," "underscores the importance of," "a key part of your home's story." A lawn is a lawn. Say what we do to it.

**2. "-ing" tail clauses.** Sentences that end with a vague participle phrase adding fake meaning: "...creating a welcoming atmosphere," "...reflecting our commitment to quality," "...ensuring lasting curb appeal," "...highlighting the beauty of your property." Cut the tail or replace it with a concrete result.

**3. TV-commercial tone.** Everything is stunning, breathtaking, lush, vibrant, pristine, immaculate, beautiful. Describe what it actually looks like instead: "even stripes," "no clippings on the driveway," "edge cut two inches deep."

**4. Copula avoidance.** Using "serves as," "stands as," "functions as," "boasts," "features" where "is" or "has" works. "Our crew serves as your trusted partner" → "Same crew every week."

**5. Negative parallelism.** "Not just X, but Y." "It's not about X, it's about Y." "No X, no Y, just Z." Max once on the whole site.

**6. Rule of three on autopilot.** "Reliable, professional, and affordable." "Mow, edge, and blow." Lists of three are fine when there are actually three things. Never pad to hit three.

**7. Editorial commentary and fake-deep closers.** Lines that tell the reader how to feel or sum up with a moral: "It's the small detail that separates a maintained lawn from a cared-for one." "Because your home deserves the best." Cut them.

**8. Section-ending summaries.** Don't end a section by restating what it just said ("In short, we handle it all"). End on the last real point or the CTA.

**9. Essay transitions.** Moreover, furthermore, additionally, in addition, on the other hand, ultimately, overall. A website isn't a school essay. Cut them.

**10. Vague attributions.** "Homeowners love...", "Many people say...", "Experts recommend..." Either quote a real review or drop it.

**11. Synonym cycling.** Calling the same thing "your yard," then "your outdoor space," then "your property," then "your landscape" to avoid repeating words. Pick the word a customer would use ("yard," "lawn," "beds") and repeat it.

**12. Bragging about coverage or online presence.** "Featured in local media outlets," "recognized across the North Shore," "maintains an active social media presence," "has shaped conversations about lawn care." Social links go in the footer, not in a sentence. If there's real press, name it once with the link.

**13. Fake "why this matters."** "This distinction matters because...", "setting the stage for...", "marking a shift in...", "deeply rooted in our community," "left an indelible mark." Just state the fact.

**14. Slogan cadence.** Punchy fragments stacked for effect: "One crew. Same faces. Every week." "Your yard, handled." Rewriting AI copy often produces this, and it reads just as fake. Write normal sentences. A little uneven is fine. Real people don't engineer every line.

**15. Formatting tells.**
- Headings in sentence case, not Title Case Like This.
- Bold only for things a skimmer truly needs. Not every other phrase.
- No emojis in body copy.
- Em dashes: max one per paragraph. Prefer a period, comma, or colon.

## Self-check before finishing
1. Read each paragraph out loud. Would Marcus actually say this to a customer standing in their driveway? If not, rewrite.
2. Search your output for every banned word, every "-ing" tail, and every em dash. Fix them.
3. Does every claim have a real fact behind it? If not, placeholder it.
4. Is there at least one concrete, local, or physical detail per section?
5. Did you just swap words, or did you actually make the sentence say something? If you only swapped words, rewrite it.

## Examples
Plain beats polished. The "after" should sound like someone explaining the job, not writing an ad.

Before: "Our meticulous edging services elevate your landscape, creating crisp, clean lines that transform your property's curb appeal."
After: "We edge along the driveway and sidewalk every time we mow."

Before: "Mulching plays a vital role in your garden's health, retaining moisture and suppressing weeds, ensuring vibrant beds all season long."
After: "We can do brown or black mulch. Most people get it done in the spring."

Before: "MD Mowing isn't just a lawn service — it's a partner in your home's beauty."
After: "I started MD Mowing in 2023 and I'm still at every job."
